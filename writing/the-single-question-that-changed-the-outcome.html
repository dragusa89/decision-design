The Single Question That Changed the Outcome

Ádám Drágus · October 2026

Someone asked me recently what the one question is. He had assumed Decision Design was a collection of techniques — and that somewhere inside it sat a single question that flips a client's perception of a project on the spot. He wanted to know what it was.

There isn't one. No question works in every room, with every client, on every project. But there are moments in every design process where a single question can change the direction of everything that follows. The skill is not memorising the question. It is recognising the moment.

This topic has no floor. What follows is one thread pulled from it.

The pull

You know the moment before you can name it. Somewhere in the work, you feel your aim being dragged sideways. Not by the brief, not by the material — by the client.

It is strange to talk about a path when design happens in the unknown. There is no path, only the one you can picture. That is exactly why it is so hard to hold. You are the only person who can see it. The client sees his own route to the goal, and to him it is just as real.

If there is enough trust in your account, he follows yours. If there isn't, he starts to pull. That is fine. You are designing for someone; their influence belongs in the work. The problem starts when the influence stops bending the path and starts replacing it. Past that point, you lose the client's direction and your own.

That is the moment. And it asks you to decide what you will do.

First, assume you're missing something

Before anything else, check yourself. The pull may be a signal that you don't understand something — about the client, his context, the pressures on him you can't see.

Dig. Ask why, then why again, then why a third time. By the third answer you usually reach the source: the real reason he wants to go this way. Often the disagreement dissolves right there, because the thing he was protecting was never the thing you were arguing about.

That is the first level.

When you understand and still disagree

You know why he wants what he wants. And you still believe it is the wrong move.

The instinct is to convince. You have the tools for it: data, best practice, benchmarks, the plain statement that you are the designer and should be trusted. There are sophisticated sales methods built for exactly this moment.

They can work but that is a trap. Persuasion is not evergreen. When the effect of your argument wears off, the client drifts back to what he believed before — and now he is living with a design he was talked into. His affection for the work you hand over will not last, because it was never his.

Change the question, not the mind

So stop trying to change his mind. Change what he is thinking about.

Not with "why". Why puts anyone on the defensive. Ask someone why he put on that shirt and he hears that his shirt is weird. Why asks him to justify a decision already made.

Move the question off the decision and onto its consequences. What outcome do you expect this to achieve? How do you think this will affect the product in the long run?

These questions open a thought experiment. He takes his own idea and runs it forward, in his head, through his own user flow. He tastes his own medicine before anyone asks him to swallow it. Whatever he finds, he found it. Nobody told him.

Turn it into a bet

Sometimes he runs the experiment and still wants his way.

Say the argument is about a button. He wants it bigger, and in an awkward place, because he believes his audience will respond to it. You know it will hurt conversion. You have told him so. It doesn't land.

So ask: would you bet your conversion rate on this? Would you bet the performance of the product — and through it, the future of the business — on this decision? The conversation changes shape. It is no longer your taste against his. It is his money on the table, and he is the one holding the chips.

Rarely does anyone lay down their weapons at this point. Agreeing outright would cost him face, and you never want a client to lose face. But more often than not, the door opens a little.

Watch for the sign: he starts asking you questions. Why do you think this is so dangerous? He can ask why — he is the client, and it is not a two-way street. The moment he asks, the arguments you held back at the start finally have somewhere to land. They are invited now, not imposed.

From there, three things can happen. He agrees. He disagrees. Or he asks for more information — and you go and get it.

This is not Netflix

Don't expect decisions on demand. A mind rarely changes in the same meeting where the question was asked.

Once the door is open, leave it open. Put the topic in the parking lot: this is one part of the design, let's move to something else, and come back to it next time. A day, two days. Give the thought room to work on him without you in the room.

He gets to change his mind privately, which costs him nothing. And when you return, the decision he makes is his own.

That is the closest thing to a single question there is. Not a sentence you can memorise, but a moment you learn to feel — and the patience to let the right question finish its work after you've asked it.
