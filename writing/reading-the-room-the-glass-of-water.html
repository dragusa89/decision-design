Reading the Room, Part One: The Glass of Water

Ádám Drágus · July 2026

Before a client says anything real, the room has already told you how the project will go. You only have to know where to look.

The Glass of Water

The first thing I notice, every time, is whether they offer me something to drink. Water, coffee, anything. It seems like nothing — a minor courtesy, half-unconscious. It is not nothing. It is the first and cleanest signal of how they have categorised you.

If they offer you a glass of water, they are treating you as a guest. And a guest is someone you have a relationship with, someone whose presence matters. If they offer nothing and move straight to the problem, you are being treated as a tool — something brought in to produce a result. The person who does not think to care for your basic comfort is telling you, without meaning to, how much room there will be for a real connection. Guest or tool: the glass of water answers it before the meeting begins.

The Wider Read

Once you are tuned to it, the signals are everywhere, and they cost nothing to observe.

If you are meeting at a café, notice what they drive, and whether they arrived on time. Notice the watch. Notice where they choose to sit, and how they arrange themselves at the table. If you are in their office, read the space: is it composed and tidy, or cluttered and chaotic, a room that feels on top of its work or buried under it? None of these is a verdict on its own. Together they start to sketch how this person sees themselves, how they hold their world, and how they are likely to hold your work.

You are not judging. You are reading.

The Client Who Won't Sit

Here is one worth learning to catch. They wave you into a seat — sit anywhere, it does not matter — but they themselves do not sit. They stay standing. They move around the room. They keep talking, and they are looking down at you the whole time.

That arrangement has meaning. Someone running that hot is usually under real pressure, high energy with nowhere to put it — and they want you seated and listening while they narrate the problem. But there is something underneath the stress, too. By keeping you down and staying up, they have placed themselves above you. Literally. The room now has a hierarchy in it, and it was built before the conversation started.

Head Height Is the Whole Game

The reason that standing bothers you — even when you cannot name it — is that head height is the entire game. Whoever's head is higher holds the dominant position. It is true in jiu-jitsu, where the fight is largely about who is on top. It is true in a meeting room. It is true in the animal kingdom — watch how dogs establish rank by getting over one another, putting the head above. The standing, pacing client is running the same primal display, in a blazer, whether they know it or not.

Once you see it this way, you stop reading the standing client as merely stressed and start reading it as a status move. And a status move can be answered.

The Window

So what do you do when someone is on their feet, facing the room, looking down at you and listing their problems? You do not stay pinned in the chair. But you also do not simply stand up and start talking — that reads as a direct challenge, and now you are in a confrontation you did not choose.

Instead, walk to the window.

There is always a reason to. You are thinking. You are considering what they have said. Getting up to look out of a window is socially invisible — nobody has ever found it strange that a person walked to a window while turning something over in their mind. You look out for a moment, showing you are genuinely weighing the problem. Then you turn, calmly, slowly, lean against the frame, and begin to speak — now at the same height, level with them.

You have changed nothing about the conversation except the geometry, and the geometry was the conversation. I would bet my neck on what happens next: the pacing stops. The looking-down stops. Without a word about hierarchy, you have relocated it. You are level, you are calm, and now you can actually work.

This Is Only the Beginning

Reading the room is a deep well, and the glass of water is only the first sip. There are countless stories in how people arrange themselves, hold themselves, and move through a space — what you can read in a handshake, a seating choice, a pause, a posture. This is Part One. There will be more, because there is far more to say about what a room tells you before anyone has decided to tell you anything.

For now, start with the water. Then watch the heads.
