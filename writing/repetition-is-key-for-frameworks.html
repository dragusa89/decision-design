Knowing something and knowing it under pressure are two different kinds.

Ádám Drágus · August 2026

I have spent years in martial arts. Boxing, Thai boxing, Krav Maga, Capoeira, and now Brazilian Jiu-jitsu. Different disciplines, but every one of them taught me the same lesson — and it turned out to be a lesson about far more than fighting.

When you learn a combination on the bag, or drill a technique with a willing partner, it is hard at first, and then it gets smoother. After enough repetition the movement becomes yours. You can throw it clean, in order, without thinking. You feel like you know it.

(Yes, this is an article about design. Bear with me.)

Then you spar for the first time. A real opponent, actively working against you, and everything goes out the window. The combination you owned an hour ago has vanished. You stand there swinging like you never learned a thing.

That is when I understood knowing something in a calm state and knowing it under pressure are two entirely different kinds of knowing.

Now what if I say that this happens to you too in every meeting.

The framework that vanishes in the room

We learn frameworks all the time. In workshops, in books, in a Decision Design session. Ask the question underneath the instruction. Don't defend the work — redirect. Reach for the source before you respond.

At your desk, calm, it makes perfect sense. You could explain it to someone else.

Then you are in the meeting. The stakeholder pushes back, harder than expected. The room is watching. And the framework is gone. You knew exactly what to do, and now you are defending a hex code like every instinct you were trying to unlearn.

That is not a failure of understanding. It is the sparring problem. You learned the move against a compliant partner and met it, for the first time, against a resisting one. Nobody performs a new technique under live pressure on the first attempt. Not in a fight, not in a meeting, not in design.

Don't run the whole sequence. Hold the first step.

Here is what actually builds the bridge — and it is not more pressure. Pressure does not teach you the technique. What it lets you do is find the one piece of it you can still hold on to.

You will not land the whole combination under pressure at first. So do not try. Dissect it. Remember the first step only.

Not the entire redirect — just the opening question. Not the whole choreography of a difficult conversation — just the single move that starts it. In grappling terms: you may not finish the submission, but you can get a grip on the arm. That is one step, and it is manageable.

Next time, you hold the first step and reach for the second. Then the third. You keep breaking the thing down into pieces small enough to survive the pressure, and you execute them one at a time.

And one day you realise you are doing the whole sequence under pressure without noticing. It assembled itself, quietly, from the steps.

Rehearse before the round

This is the part I see skipped most of the time, and it is the most practical move in the whole piece. When you prepare for a meeting, you prepare the brief. You prepare the concept, the screens, the rationale for the design itself. You rehearse the work.

You almost never rehearse the conversation.

But the conversation is the round you are walking into. So prepare the Decision Design part the same way a fighter warms up the combination before sparring. Before the meeting, run it: what is the first question I want to ask, in what order, based on what logic? Five minutes is enough.

Do that consistently and something shifts. The five-minute rehearsal stops being a thing you need. The move is simply there when the pressure arrives, the way a trained combination is there without being summoned. That is the whole point of repetition — to move the knowledge from the part of you that has to think to the part of you that just acts.

Owning the move is where the work begins

There is one more stage, and it starts once you learn the new approach.

Once a combination survives real pressure, you start to change it. You find your own tweaks — a small adjustment in timing, a grip that suits your build, your reach, your weight. The version in the textbook was a general recipe written for nobody in particular. The version that actually works is the one you have personalised until it fits you and no one else.

The same is true of every framework. The workshop gives you the general recipe: ask the question, redirect the objection. But your delivery, your wording, your instinct for the exact moment to use it — that is yours to build. A question phrased in someone else's voice sounds like a script. The same question in your own words sounds like you.

And here is the catch that ties the whole thing together: you can only personalise a technique under pressure. You cannot discover your own version of a move on the bag, alone, in comfort. You find it in the live round, when a real opponent forces you to adapt. You find your own version of a framework in the real meeting, with a real client pushing back — never in the calm of preparation.

So the goal was never to memorise the framework. It was to repeat it, step by step, under real conditions, long enough that it stops being something you learned and becomes automatic.

Then you can start to influence it. Mold it further.

That is when it is finally yours and starts to evolve even more.
