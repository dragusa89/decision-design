Transfer of Value

Ádám Drágus · October 2026

Design happens in the unknown. You work from hypothesis, experience and judgement, and what you make is not yet anything. A concept, a prototype, a product — none of it is real until someone uses it. Only then does it turn out to be good or bad.

Which means there is a window, short and dangerous, where the only thing standing around the newly born thing is your own conviction. Nothing else holds it up. Not data, not proof, not results. Just your belief, built on your experience and your knowledge of the client.

And in that window, you have to hand it over.

The narrow gap

The transfer is fragile for a reason that has nothing to do with skill.

If I could copy the idea from my head into my client's head exactly as it exists in mine, there would be no room left for him to internalise it. Nothing to attach to, nothing to make his own. It would not be memorable, and in a strange way it would not even be understandable.

And if I leave too much space, the message distorts. It arrives as something else, or it doesn't arrive at all. The concept shatters before it is ever used.

So the work sits in a narrow gap between those two failures. Too little room and there is nothing to hold onto. Too much and there is nothing left of the idea. The craft is deciding how wide to leave the opening.

Mother

Elon Musk described something on a podcast once, talking about Neuralink, that has an uncomfortably good parallel with design.

If I say the word mother, everyone knows what I mean. A woman who gave life to another being. The word transfers perfectly; nobody misunderstands it.

But when I say mother, I am thinking about my mother. Not only how she looks or how she acts, but everything attached to her — the way she raised me, what I owe her, what I never resolved with her. Someone else hears the same word and loads their own. The word is identical. The contents are entirely private.

That is what Neuralink is promising to solve: skip the lossy word, transmit the thing itself. It is exactly the thing a designer cannot do. I can never think with my client's head. I cannot produce the same small electrical impulses in his brain that are firing in mine.

Every concept you hand over works the same way. You say the design feels premium, or that it works, or that it is simple. The words arrive intact. The meaning is rebuilt on the other side out of materials you have never seen.

Perfect transfer would kill design

Here is the catch. If I could do it — if I could transmit the idea whole and unaltered — I would have killed the thing I was trying to deliver.

Good design needs real estate around it. Free space. The only way it becomes personal to the viewer is if there is room for him to walk in with his own experiences and stories and attach them to it. Without that freedom of movement in his mind, it is not design. It is copy-paste.

And what you would be pasting is something that is only true for you.

That is the part worth sitting with. Even if the client ended up with a flawless copy of your perception, it would still be your perception living in his head. Borrowed, not built. It would hold exactly as long as you were standing next to it, and no longer. Persuasion is a loan. So is a perfect copy.

What you actually control

So you stop trying to transmit the whole thing, and you start deciding what to close and what to leave open.

Some things must be fixed, because if they move, the concept collapses. The problem you are solving. Who it is for. What has to be true for it to work. What you deliberately chose not to do. These are the load-bearing walls, and they need to be stated in language that does not leave room for a second reading.

Everything else is where the client gets to move. What it reminds him of. Where he sees it in his business. What he imagines his customers doing with it. You are not there to fill those in. You are there to leave them open and watch what he puts in them. Which is also your best feedback.

When a client tells you what he sees in the work, he is showing you the gap between what you transferred and what arrived. If what he describes is close enough to hold, the transfer worked. If it has drifted somewhere you never intended, you know exactly where the opening was too wide — and you can narrow it before the next conversation, not after the project has gone sideways.

The handover is the design

It is tempting to treat the transfer as the last step: the work is done, now go explain it. It isn't. The moment the concept moves from your head to someone else's is the moment it becomes vulnerable, and how you manage that moment determines whether anything survives the trip.

You are handing over something unproven, held up by your conviction, into a mind you cannot enter — and leaving precisely enough room for that mind to make it its own.

Get the gap right and the idea keeps standing after you leave the room. That is the transfer of value.
